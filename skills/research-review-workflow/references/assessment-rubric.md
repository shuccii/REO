# Assessment rubric

These ten dimensions are coverage axes, not an official grading system. Use supported, limited, unsupported, invalidated or not assessed for claims. “Unsupported” means the inspected material does not justify the claim; “invalidated” requires a demonstrated defect undermining it. A request for more evidence is not proof of error. Scores, if requested, require explicit anchors and confidence and cannot determine degree eligibility.

| Dimension ID | Decisions and questions | Evidence that can change the assessment |
|---|---|---|
| question_significance | Is the scientific/practical problem concrete? Is the goal testable, with a measurable success/failure condition? Why does solving it matter and for whom? Is scope feasible at this stage? | Problem statement, target definition, success criterion, timeline and actual use conditions |
| novelty_literature | What is known, disputed and still missing? Which closest studies differ in task, data, assumptions, method or knowledge gained? Distinguish a new application/negative result/new insight from a new algorithm and an unsupported “first.” | Primary prior-art comparison, reproducible search scope/date, strongest baseline and contrary literature |
| logic_claims | Do background, objective, method, figures and conclusions form a warranted argument? Which premise connects observation to conclusion? Is the argument circular, equivocal, or selectively reported? | Exact claim/figure locators, derivations, counterexample, alternative explanation and boundary conditions |
| design_identifiability | Can the design distinguish the intended explanations? Are controls, comparison conditions and independence appropriate? What confounding, reverse causation, selection or unobserved factors remain? | Design/sampling protocol, control/ablation, causal assumptions when relevant, falsification experiment |
| data_measurement | What was measured/defined, where and under what conditions? Are units, provenance, calibration, missingness, exclusions, duplicates and selection bias accounted for? Distinguish technical replicates from independent samples. | Raw/source data, sampling/measurement protocol, calibration and quality/uncertainty record |
| statistics_uncertainty | Do effect size, interval, sample unit and analysis support the claim? Which sources of variability do repeats capture? Are multiplicity, tuning and dependence handled? Do diagnostics fit the assumptions? | Recomputable outcomes, matched comparisons, effect/interval estimates, residual/sensitivity/calibration checks |
| domain_validity | Are mathematical assumptions and physical/chemical constraints coherent? Is prediction feasible and relevant under stated conditions? Are mechanisms demonstrated or only suggested? | Units, limits, constraints, independent measurement/theory, applicability domain and uncertainty |
| reproducibility | Can an independent reader reconstruct the procedure and reported values? Are versions, parameters, seeds, environment and exceptions known? Could two correct implementations disagree? | Commands, data/code/config identity, raw outputs, metric definitions, executable reproduction or explicit restrictions |
| communication_defense | Can another-field examiner follow the problem, contribution and evidence? Are definitions and conditions explicit? Do figures, text, tables and oral answers agree? Can the author justify choices and limits? | Versioned thesis/slides, figure captions/axes, reconciliation of counts/metrics, evidence-backed Q&A |
| ethics_governance | Are citations, attribution, data permissions, privacy, contribution and conflicts handled? Are approval/status boundaries accurately stated? | Source attribution, license/consent where relevant, applicable local policies, documented approvals |

## Cross-cutting reasoning

- Separate observation, inference, hypothesis and proposal. Identify whether a claim is descriptive, predictive, causal, mechanistic, mathematical or prospective.
- Check necessity and sufficiency: an accurate prediction, convergence, a correlation, a plausible narrative or a citation does not alone establish a mechanism or universal validity.
- Test alternative hypotheses: artifact, batch/source effect, confounding, cherry-picking, metric choice, smoothing or selection. Name only alternatives plausible for the actual design, not arbitrary doubt.
- Inspect falsifiability and identifiability. If two explanations predict the same available observation, propose a measurement where they disagree or narrow the claim.
- State what would change the judgment. New evidence may resolve or strengthen a finding; document the change and its locator.
- Preserve useful contributions under a narrower claim. A negative result, reproducible benchmark or bounded mechanistic insight may be valuable without universal superiority.

NAIST's publicly described master's criteria map to background/objective, method scrutiny, result organization/analysis, logical conclusion/development, scholarly writing and ethics; novelty and usefulness are emphasized. This rubric broadens operational checks without inventing official thresholds. Institution/venue criteria and the author's research stage determine expectations.

## Explicit validity and thinking checks

Distinguish construct validity (does the measured proxy represent the intended concept?), internal validity (can the proposed explanation be identified against confounding/alternative designs?), statistical conclusion validity (do dependence, uncertainty and analysis justify the inference?) and external validity (which populations/materials/conditions generalize?). This operational distinction is a synthesis, not an institutional grading rule.

For each consequential argument expose claim, evidence, inferential warrant, backing for that warrant, scope qualifier and plausible rebuttal. Check clarity, factual accuracy, precision, relevance, depth, breadth, consistency, significance and fair treatment of contrary evidence. Unsupported warrants become specific evidence requests, not generic criticism.

For supervision, adapt DARPA's Heilmeier questions: goal in plain language; current practice/limits; what changes and why it may work; who benefits; risks; cost; time; measurable mid-term and final checks. This helps operationalize feasibility without turning a research thesis into a commercial business case.
